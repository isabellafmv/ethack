The factors any investor checks before a sustainability thesis even enters the conversation, alongside our own Sustainability Score, and a rule-based answer to how much a $1B fund would actually put behind each name. Computed per company from Isabella's pipeline export (data/wide_FY2025_fallback.csv) — no new data pulled for this page.

Valuationcomputed

Is it cheap or expensive relative to cash flow? Shown as FCF Yield (free cash flow ÷ market cap) — the best transition story at a bubble valuation is still a bad trade.

Financial qualitypartial

Shown as EBITDA margin and FCF margin — profitability and cash conversion. True leverage (debt/EBITDA) and ROIC aren't shown: no debt or invested-capital data is in the current pipeline.

Free cash flow generationcomputed

Raw free cash flow in dollars, plus FCF margin above. Cash pays for the transition (or doesn't) — this is the same field transition_affordability_score already depends on.

Growth trajectorycomputed

Shown as revenue CAGR, 2021→2025 — pulled straight from the database's multi-year history, which the standard export collapses to one pinned fiscal year. Caveat: a few names (mostly banks) show implausible spikes from an XBRL revenue-tag inconsistency across years, not real growth — spot-check extreme values.

Competitive moatnot available

Market share stability and pricing power are qualitative — nothing in a structured financial-statement pipeline captures this; would need new research, not a new field.

Liquiditypartial

Shown as market cap — a coarse proxy for how large a position a $1B fund could actually build. True average-daily-volume data isn't pulled.

Volatility / correlationnot available

Needs historical stock price data (for beta, drawdown, cross-holding correlation) — no price time series exists in the current pipeline, only fundamentals.

How much to invest — you set the rule

Risk, without price datacomputed

No volatility, beta, or price history exists in this pipeline — so Risk Score is a fundamentals-only proxy, the mean of whichever of three signals a company has (same renormalized-average pattern as every pillar score in this project): size (smaller market cap → historically more volatile), earnings instability (standard deviation of year-over-year revenue growth, 2021–2025 — the same multi-year history behind Revenue CAGR above), and fragility (thin or negative FCF margin → less room to absorb a shock). Higher score = riskier. It's a real proxy for risk, not a substitute for one — treat it as directional, not precise.

Diversification, by count and sector — not by capcomputed

A per-name dollar cap almost never binds once conviction is spread across a few hundred companies, so it was dropped as a diversification lever. Instead: number of holdings directly controls concentration (fewer names, each larger, vs. more names, each smaller), and max allocation per sector directly stops any one sector from quietly dominating the book — the two things that actually determine whether "diversified" is true or just a label.
